## Black Metal Chords, Scales, Harmonies, and Progressions

Black metal harmony centers on **full minor triads**, **power chords** (including flattened-fifth **tritone** and **augmented** variants), **diminished** and **augmented triads**, **diminished seventh (`dim7`)** and **added-ninth (`add9`)** extensions, and **open-string pedal voicings** that generate minor-second clashes. Its core scale vocabulary consists of **Aeolian (Natural Minor)**, **Harmonic Minor**, **Phrygian**, **Locrian**, and **chromaticism**, supplemented in extreme-metal dissonance practices by **Octatonic (Half-Whole Diminished)**, **Augmented**, and **Harmonic Minor modes**. Harmonically, the genre diverges from conventional heavy metal by exploiting **distortion-amplified combination tones** across minor triads, **non-diatonic minor-chord planing**, **chromatic mediants**, **Weitzmann-region voice leading**, and **internal drone strings**. Progressions span from hypnotic **Aeolian minor vamps** (`i–bVII` and `i–bVI–bVII`) to **four-chord non-diatonic ostinati** elevated in **`i`**, **`iii` / `#iii`**, **`vi`**, and **`#vii`** minor triads alongside **chromatic and tritone shifts**.

A defining feature of this harmonic vocabulary is **subgenre divergence**. Second-wave Norwegian, melodic, atmospheric, and dungeon-synth-adjacent styles frequently rely on Aeolian or Harmonic Minor tonal centers, power chords, and harmonized lines, whereas depressive suicidal black metal (DSBM) and dissonant third-wave bands often abandon functional key signatures for chromatic mediants, Weitzmann cycles, and atonal intervals. Post-black metal further widens the harmonic field by mixing stricter minor melodies with clean-guitar extensions and major-key progressions.

Evidence across these traditions carries clear methodological caveats. Peer-reviewed corpus work by Jamie Boddington Jordan and Jan-Peter Herbst—analyzing 100 post-2000 metal tracks across five subgenres, including 20 black metal tracks—provides the strongest quantitative foundation, though full percentage tables across all 24 triad classes and the complete 20-track appendix list are not itemized in the published summaries of that dataset. Where academic transcriptions are absent, guitar tutorials, forum theory discussions, and automated chord-recognition platforms supply practical examples, though automated chord estimators regularly misread two-note power chords and linear tremolo riffs as complete triads.

## Core Chords and Guitar Voicings

Heavy metal musicology—from Robert Walser to Harris Berger—traditionally treats the two-note **power chord** (`1–5`) as metal's baseline harmonic unit because omitting the third allows distortion to reinforce the root cleanly without dissonant triadic roughness. While black metal retains power chords for high-speed tremolo picking, studies by Ross Hagen, Jan-Peter Herbst, and Jamie Boddington Jordan demonstrate that black metal breaks from other metal subgenres by favoring **full triad voicings**, especially **minor chords**. Played through high-gain distortion, full minor voicings generate a denser, less resonant timbre that purposefully trades crisp note separation for harsh textural weight.

To heighten instability beyond the minor triad, black metal guitarists routinely alter the fifth of power chords, deploy symmetrical diminished sevenths and half-diminished chords, and fret movable shapes over unmuted open strings. The table below catalogs the genre's primary chord structures.

| Chord Type | Interval Formula | Typical Guitar Shapes & Execution | Harmonic Role & Prevalence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Minor Triad (`m`) | `1 – b3 – 5` | Full barre or open minor shapes; often shifted non-diatonically along the neck | Signature harmonic building block across much of black metal; yields dense spectral roughness under distortion |
| Power Chord (`5`) | `1 – 5` (or `1 – 5 – 1`) | Two- or three-string movable shapes; single-finger bars in Drop D, C, or B | Core rhythmic device for rapid tremolo picking and 2nd-wave/raw black metal ostinati |
| Tritone / Diminished Power Chord (`5b5`) | `1 – b5` | Lowering the fifth of a standard power chord by 1 fret (6 semitones) | Creates acute harmonic friction; alternated with standard `5` chords in dark/thrashy black metal |
| Diminished Triad & `dim7` | `1 – b3 – b5` (`– bb7`) | Symmetrical 4-note `dim7` shapes repeating every 3 frets (`Cdim7 = Ebdim7 = Gbdim7 = Adim7`) | Passing chords and chilling interludes; semitone leading tones inside `dim7` pull strongly toward minor targets |

## Common Scales and Modal Palettes

Black metal melody and riffing favor scales packed with **minor seconds (`m2`)** and **tritones (`TT`)**. To compare how densely each scale generates these intervals, pitch-class set theory uses an **Interval-Class Vector (ICV)** —a six-integer count `<ic1, ic2, ic3, ic4, ic5, ic6>` tallying all note pairs from minor seconds/major sevenths (`ic1`) up to tritones (`ic6`). Although all seven-note diatonic modes share the identical ICV `[2, 5, 4, 3, 6, 1]` because they are rotations of the major scale, shifting the tonal center to **Phrygian** or **Locrian** places `b2` or both `b2` and `b5` directly against the tonic. Harmonic Minor variants increase `ic1` to 3 or 4 and double `ic6` to 2, while the symmetric Octatonic scale contains four minor-second pairs and four tritone pairs.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aeolian (Natural Minor) | `[0, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10]` | `1 – 2 – b3 – 4 – 5 – b6 – b7` | `[2, 5, 4, 3, 6, 1]` | **Core:** Key-centered foundation for classic 2nd-wave, atmospheric, and dungeon-synth-adjacent black metal |
| Harmonic Minor | `[0, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 11]` | `1 – 2 – b3 – 4 – 5 – b6 – 7` | `[3, 3, 5, 4, 4, 2]` | **Core:** Paired with Natural Minor in guitar pedagogy as a top black metal mode; supplies raised 7th and `b6–7` augmented 2nd |
| Phrygian | `[0, 1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10]` | `1 – b2 – b3 – 4 – 5 – b6 – b7` | `[2, 5, 4, 3, 6, 1]` | **Core / Modal Inflection:** Sinister `b2` pull; applied globally or locally ("Phrygianizing" a riff), as noted in *"Freezing Moon"* solo discussions |

| Hungarian / Double Harmonic Minor | `[0, 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 11]` | `1 – 2 – b3 – #4 – 5 – b6 – 7` | `[4, 2, 4, 5, 4, 2]` | **Niche / Broader Metal:** Harmonic minor plus `#4` tritone; prominent in general dark/progressive metal discussions (e.g., Opeth) |
| Double Harmonic Major (Byzantine) | `[0, 1, 4, 5, 7, 8, 11]` | `1 – b2 – 3 – 4 – 5 – b6 – 7` | `[4, 2, 4, 5, 4, 2]` | **Niche / Forum-Cited:** Symmetric major counterpart to Hungarian Minor with 4 `m2` pairs |
| Whole-Tone | `[0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10]` | `1 – 2 – 3 – #4 – #5 – b7` | `[0, 6, 0, 6, 0, 3]` | **Niche / Tool-Listed:** Zero semitones or fifths (`ic1 = 0, ic5 = 0`); listed in tuning/prompt tools (including Leading Whole Tone in Mayhem tuning) |

Two stylistic contrasts sharpen these boundaries. First, **Dorian** (`1–2–b3–4–5–6–b7`)—the brightest minor mode because it produces a major `IV` chord—is statistically characteristic of **power metal** rather than black metal, though composers can still voice it darkly. Second, **pentatonic and blues scales** remain contested in musician discussions: while beginner metal guides treat Minor Pentatonic (`1–b3–4–5–b7`) as universal, extreme-metal theorists argue that dissonant black and death metal typically eschew blues clichés in favor of chromatic, Phrygian, Locrian, or diminished structures. In **post-black metal and atmospheric strands**, however, composers sometimes reintroduce strict melodic minor scales or outright **major scales**.

## Harmonic Mechanics and Distortion Psychoacoustics

### Distortion Acoustics and Combination Tones

High-gain guitar distortion compresses dynamic peaks, amplifies upper harmonic partials, and generates nonlinear **combination tones** —chiefly **difference tones** (`f2 - f1`), **summation tones** (`f1 + f2`), and **cubic difference tones** (`2f1 - f2`). Evaluating equal-tempered dyads above an open `A2` string (`f1 = 110.0 Hz`, `f2 = f1 × 2^(semitones/12)`) illustrates physically why the power chord remains acoustically transparent while black metal's minor thirds, minor seconds, and tritones turn harmony into spectral noise.

| Dyad above `A2` (`110.0 Hz`) | Semitones | Upper Pitch (`f2`) | Difference Tone (`f2 - f1`) | Cubic Difference (`2f1 - f2`) | Summation Tone (`f1 + f2`) | Acoustic Behavior Under Distortion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Minor 2nd (`Bb2`) | 1 | `116.54 Hz` | `6.54 Hz` | `103.46 Hz` | `226.54 Hz` | Slow `6.54 Hz` amplitude beating plus clashing lower `103.46 Hz` (~ `G#2`) tone |
| Major 2nd (`B2`) | 2 | `123.47 Hz` | `13.47 Hz` | `96.53 Hz` | `233.47 Hz` | Rapid `13.47 Hz` roughness and low-frequency mud |
| Minor 3rd (`C3`) | 3 | `130.81 Hz` | `20.81 Hz` | `89.19 Hz` | `240.81 Hz` | Low `20.81 Hz` modulation and non-harmonic `89.19 Hz` clash, creating minor-chord roughness |
| Major 3rd (`C#3`) | 4 | `138.59 Hz` | `28.59 Hz` | `81.41 Hz` | `248.59 Hz` | Cubic difference sits near `E2` (`82.41 Hz`), lending fifth-like harmonic support |
| Perfect 4th (`D3`) | 5 | `146.83 Hz` | `36.83 Hz` | `73.17 Hz` | `256.83 Hz` | Difference tones (`36.83 Hz`, `73.17 Hz`) shift perceived root downward toward `D` |
| Tritone (`Eb3`) | 6 | `155.56 Hz` | `45.56 Hz` | `64.44 Hz` | `265.56 Hz` | Non-harmonic `45.56 Hz` and `64.44 Hz` (~ `C2`) ghost pitches drive acute instability |
| Perfect 5th (`E3`) | 7 | `164.81 Hz` | `54.81 Hz` | `55.19 Hz` | `274.81 Hz` | Both difference tones converge near `A1` (`55.0 Hz`), reinforcing the root cleanly |
| Major 7th (`G#3`) | 11 | `207.65 Hz` | `97.65 Hz` | `12.35 Hz` | `317.65 Hz` | Produces `12.35 Hz` cubic beating and conflicting `97.65 Hz` tone |
| Minor 9th (`Bb3`) | 13 | `233.08 Hz` | `123.08 Hz` | `-13.08 Hz` | `343.08 Hz` | Piercing `123.08 Hz` (~ `B2`) difference pitch plus `13.08 Hz` inverted modulation clash |

In a justly intoned fifth (`A = 110 Hz`, `E = 165 Hz`), `3 × f1` matches `2 × f2` at `330 Hz` and the difference tone lands squarely on `A1` (`55 Hz`), explaining why power chords achieve peak perceived loudness and stability. By contrast, minor thirds (`89.19 Hz` cubic difference), tritones (`64.44 Hz`), and minor seconds (`6.54 Hz` beating and `103.46 Hz` difference pitch) populate the low spectrum with non-harmonic partials and amplitude fluctuations perceived as gritty noise.

### Neo-Riemannian Voice Leading and Weitzmann Regions

Because black metal frequently dispenses with classical dominant-to-tonic (`V7–i`) cadences, standard Roman-numeral analysis often fails to account for the cohesion of its non-diatonic loops. Boddington Jordan and Herbst apply **neo-Riemannian theory** —measuring semitone voice-leading distance ("units of work") rather than key signatures—and specifically **Weitzmann regions** (described by Carl Weitzmann in 1853 and Richard Cohn in 2012).

A Weitzmann region comprises **six consonant triads** —three minor and three major—each separated by **one semitone** from a central augmented triad. Mapping the region around `{Bb, D, F#}` (pitch classes `{10, 2, 6}`) explains the harmonic architecture of Leviathan's *"Fucking Your Ghost in Chains of Ice"* (`0:42–0:44`):

- **Raising one pitch by +1 semitone yields the three minor triads:** `Bm` (spelled `Cbm`: `{B, D, F#}`), `Ebm` (`{Eb, Gb, Bb}`), and `Gm` (`{G, Bb, D}`).
- **Lowering one pitch by -1 semitone yields the three major triads:** `D` (`{D, F#, A}`), `Gb/F#` (`{Db, Gb, Bb}`), and `Bb` (`{Bb, D, F}`).

In Leviathan's four-chord cycle (`Ebm – Cbm/Bm – Gm – Bb`), roots descend in eerie major thirds while retaining common tones across every transition (`Gb/F#` from `Ebm` to `Cbm/Bm`; `D` from `Bm` to `Gm`; both `D` and `Bb` from `Gm` to `Bb`; and `Bb` returning to `Ebm`). The concluding `Bb` major triad temporarily behaves as a dominant (`V`) of `Ebm`, cycling the uncanny ostinato smoothly back to the tonic.

### Chromatic Mediants, Parallel Planing, and Internal Pedal Tones

Closely tied to Weitzmann cycles is **chromatic mediant** motion—linking two triads of the same quality (typically minor) whose roots are a major or minor third apart. Where conventional metal moves a major third below minor tonic to a **major `VI`** chord, black metal frequently keeps that chord **minor** (as in Leviathan's `Ebm` to `Cbm` or Shining's `Bm` to `Gm`), creating what theorists term a "suspension of tonal gravity". In Shining's *"Yttligare Ett Steg Närmare Total Jävla Utfrysning"* (`0:21`), `Bm` is inverted with `F#` in the bass (`Bm/F#`) so that moving `B` down one semitone to `Bb` and `F#` up one semitone to `G` (while holding `D`) yields maximally smooth voice leading into `Gm`. At `1:15–1:18`, Shining shifts directly between `F#m` and the augmented triad anchor of its Weitzmann region.

On the guitar fretboard, musicians also sustain bleak tonal unity without strict scales through **parallel minor planing** —moving a fixed minor shape chromatically or across non-diatonic roots such as `Gm – G#m – A#m – Bm` —and **open-string internal pedals**. In a community sheet-music analysis of Burzum's *"Dunkelheit"*, an open `G` string rings continuously as a minor-third internal pedal beneath shifting chord shapes (`Em`, `Cm7`, `B5`, `F#5`, and `D#`), fusing dissonant upper voice movement to a fixed timbral anchor.

### Texture, Counterpoint, and Third-Wave Dissonance

Black metal realizes these harmonies through two primary rhythmic textures: **sustained tremolo strumming** (creating rapid chordal beds where bar-level rhythm decouples from picking speed) and **arpeggiated or contrapuntal lines**. Melodic black metal frequently applies twin-guitar harmonies stacked in diatonic thirds or fifths, whereas avant-garde and dissonant third-wave bands layer complex, jagged counterpoint across alternate tunings. Musicologist Wolf-Georg Zaddach highlights Deathspell Omega's *"Wings of Predation"* (`0:00–0:06`, with all instruments down-tuned a whole tone) and Imperial Triumphant's *"Rotted Futures"* (`1:44–2:02`, using drop-D/open tuning) and *"City Swine"* (`6:09–6:16`) as landmarks of third-wave intervallic dissonance, while guitarists such as Kevin Hufnagel cite Blut Aus Nord's Vindsval for balancing consonance and extreme dissonance in *"MoRT Chapter II"* —contrasting sharply with Reddit commentary that groups Blut Aus Nord with Darkthrone and Emperor as strictly Aeolian.

## Common Chord Progressions and Quantitative Corpus Evidence

Academic corpus research overturns earlier claims—such as Owen Coggins's (2019) description of predictable one-chord-per-bar loops and Stabler's (2016) characterization of black metal as a "musical cage of flattened supertonics and parallel fifth chords". Across Boddington Jordan and Herbst's 100-track post-2000 corpus (20 tracks per subgenre across power metal, black metal, metalcore, melodic death metal, and progressive metal), **black metal** and progressive metal were the only subgenres to employ **all 24 major and minor triads**.

Black metal tracks typically construct **four-chord harmonic ostinati** beginning on the minor tonic `i`, frequently shifting tonal centers between sections rather than relying on classical cadences. ANOVA testing with post-hoc Tukey HSD comparisons across the five subgenres established four statistically significant triad signatures in black metal (note that in the study's Roman-numeral system, minor-key tonic `i` contrasts with raised minor `iii` / `#iii`, flattened submediant minor `vi` rather than major `VI`, and raised leading-tone minor `#vii`):

- **`i` (Minor Tonic):** Black metal differed significantly from all subgenres except melodic death metal (`p < 0.01`; partial eta-squared = `0.17`), anchoring progressions on minor tonics.
- **`iii` / `#iii`:** Significantly more prevalent in black metal and progressive metal than in other subgenres (`p < 0.05`; partial eta-squared = `0.12`). In Jordan's thesis data, the non-diatonic **`#iii`** minor triad appeared in **25%** of black metal tracks—more than five times as often as in any other subgenre.
- **`vi`:** Minor chords a major third below tonic (instead of metal's standard major `VI`) appeared significantly more often in black metal (`p < 0.01`; partial eta-squared = `0.18`), occurring in **15%** of black metal tracks.
- **`#vii`:** Employed significantly more often in black metal than in all subgenres except progressive metal (`p < 0.01`; partial eta-squared = `0.17`). By contrast, the major counterparts of `#iii` and `vi` appeared only **once each** in the black metal corpus.

## Guitar Tunings and Voicing Clarity in Practice

Second-wave Norwegian classics by Darkthrone, Mayhem, and Emperor are predominantly played in standard `E–A–D–G–B–E` tuning, where open treble strings (`G`, `B`, `E`) facilitate minor-third pedals and cold upper-register dissonance. Other dark and third-wave acts adopt lowered or alternate tunings—such as whole-step down-tuning in Deathspell Omega's *"Wings of Predation"*, drop-D/open configurations in Imperial Triumphant's *"Rotted Futures"*, Drop D (`D–A–D–G–B–E`), Drop C, Drop B, or niche open tunings like `E–A–C#–G#–A–F` —to extend bass weight or enable single-finger fifths and unorthodox open drones.

Because minor triads and diminished chords generate dense combination tones under high gain, achieving harmonic definition requires deliberate tone management. Production guides note that excessive distortion or deeply "scooped" midranges smear minor thirds and diminished sevenths into indistinct wash; preserving midrange presence (`800 Hz–2 kHz`), upper-mid bite (`1–3 kHz`), and pick-attack presence (`4–6 kHz`) lets individual chord tones articulate, while cutting `200–500 Hz` low-mid buildup and applying a high-pass filter near `80–120 Hz` (especially on extended-range or down-tuned guitars) prevents dense voicings from clashing with the bass. Recording tight active or high-output passive pickups—or layering an upper-octave minor or diminished chord above a simple low-register power chord—allows black metal's complex non-diatonic harmony to remain audible without sacrificing raw weight.
